# swift-geometry

Use this package when you compute with shapes in a coordinate space: points, lines, segments, rays, circles, ellipses, arcs, Bézier curves, polygons, rectangles and paths, and regions known by their signed distance. Start from `Geometry<Scalar, Space>` and its nested types, such as `Geometry.Point`, `Geometry.Rectangle`, `Geometry.Polygon` and `Geometry.Path`. A `Geometry.Path` can be moved by an affine map (into another `Space` if you say so), flattened to lines, asked how often it winds around a point, and stroked into the outline of a pen line. `Space` is a tag that keeps values from different spaces apart. The shapes come with the `Affine` trait; without it the package holds only sizes, depths and magnitudes. If you need bare points or vectors and no shapes, use swift-point or swift-linear.

## Laws

- The y axis points up. A rectangle is given by its lower-left and upper-right corners, `Geometry.Direction.up` is positive y, and a positive sweep or a positive signed area means counterclockwise.
- Nothing translates a shape implicitly. Translation takes an explicit `Affine` relationship, such as `Geometry.Point.cartesian`.
- A rectangle stores its center and half extents. With integer scalars, an odd width or height is rounded down to an even one.
- Rectangles, squares and circles contain the points on their boundary. `Geometry.Ball.containsInterior(_:)` excludes them. Rectangles that only touch intersect, and their intersection has zero width or height.
- `Geometry.Polygon.contains(_:)` and `Geometry.Ngon.contains(_:)` use the even-odd rule, so a point exactly on an edge can fall either way. `Geometry.Polygon.isOnBoundary(_:)` answers that case, within 100 times `ulpOfOne`.
- Parallel lines, rays and segments have no intersection. The test is exact, with no tolerance, and collinear overlapping segments have none either.
- `Geometry.Polygon.triangulate()` clips ears from counterclockwise vertices and stops when it finds none. Clockwise or self-intersecting input therefore gives fewer triangles than the vertex count minus two, possibly none.
- Converting an arc or an elliptical arc to Bézier curves gives one cubic segment per started quarter turn. A sweep of 2.5π gives five segments and ends at the arc's own end point.
- That conversion gives no segments when the start angle, end angle or sweep is not finite, when the sweep is zero, or when more than `Geometry.arcBezierSegmentLimit` segments (4096) would be needed. These checks run before anything is allocated.
- `Geometry.Ellipse.Arc.init(from:to:rx:ry:xAxisRotation:largeArcFlag:sweepFlag:)` follows SVG's endpoint form. Radii too small to reach both points are scaled up. Identical endpoints or a zero radius give an arc with zero sweep, which converts to no Bézier segments.
- A closed subpath converted to Bézier curves gets a closing line only when its last segment does not already end at the start point.
- `Geometry.Ball.bezierCurves` runs clockwise from the rightmost point of the circle. `Geometry.Bezier.approximating(_:)` runs counterclockwise from the same point.
- Bézier and elliptical arc lengths are measured along 100 straight pieces by default. An ellipse's perimeter uses Ramanujan's approximation.
- A path is read as continuous. Where a segment does not start at the point the one before it ended, or the first segment at the subpath's start point, a straight line joins the two. Flattening and stroking close a gap no longer than their tolerance by carrying on from the earlier point. A segment that converts to no Bézier curve adds nothing: an arc with no sweep, with an angle that is not finite or with more than `Geometry.arcBezierSegmentLimit` quarter turns, and a Bézier with fewer than two control points.
- `Geometry.Path(_:radius:)` is a rectangle's border as one closed subpath, counterclockwise as the names have it: from (llx, lly) along the lower side to (urx, lly), up, back along the upper side and down. With a radius, each corner is a quarter-circle `arc` of that radius that touches both sides, its centre the radius in from each, and the subpath starts where the lower side leaves the first corner's arc, at (llx + radius, lly): four sides and four arcs in turn. The radius is held to half the shorter side, where the two arcs at each end of that side meet and the side between them has no length. A radius that is not positive, or not a number, gives the four sides alone. `Geometry.Path(_:)` of a circle is one closed subpath holding `Geometry.Arc.fullCircle`, counterclockwise from the circle's rightmost point. An arc's ends come from its angles' cosine and sine, so a side and the arc after it can differ in the last bits; a path is read as continuous, as above.
- `Geometry.Path(_:lowerLeft:lowerRight:upperRight:upperLeft:)` gives each corner its own radius, each held to half the shorter side; a corner whose radius is not positive, or not a number, is square and has no arc. The subpath starts at (llx + lowerLeft, lly) and runs as above. With one radius for every corner it is `Geometry.Path(_:radius:)`.
- `Geometry.Path.loop(through:)` is the closed uniform Catmull–Rom spline through the vertices in order: one closed subpath from the first vertex, one cubic `bezier` from each vertex to the next, the last back to the first. The control points are each vertex plus and minus a sixth of the chord between its neighbours, so the curve's tangent is continuous at every vertex. Fewer than three vertices give `nil`.
- `Geometry.Star` is a regular star of at least two points (`init?` gives `nil` for fewer): points at `outer` from its center, a notch at `inner` halfway between each two, joined by straight edges, the first point on the positive x axis. `radius(at:)` is the exact distance from the center to that edge in a direction, `outer` toward a point and `inner` toward a notch, repeating with each point and the same on either side of it; with `inner` at `outer` times the cosine of π / points it is the regular polygon. `vertices` alternate point and notch counterclockwise, and `Geometry.Path(_:)` of a star is one closed subpath of their edges. The number of points is a `Cardinal`; a star encodes it as a bare count beside `center`, `outer` and `inner`, and fails to decode with fewer than two.
- `Geometry.Path(_:winding:)` of a sequence of polygons is one closed subpath of straight edges for each polygon of at least three vertices, from its first vertex and in the order given, as `polygon(vertices:)` makes it; a polygon of fewer adds nothing. With a `Winding` of swift-direction, a polygon whose signed area has the other sign is walked backwards from the same first vertex, and one of zero area is kept as it is. Every simple polygon then winds once around its inside in the same sense, so under `nonzero` the path fills the union of the polygons however they overlap, and under `evenodd` the overlaps are holes. A self-intersecting polygon has parts of both senses whichever way it runs.
- `Geometry.Radial` is a closed curve given by its radius r(θ) in each direction, θ counterclockwise from the positive x axis, about no centre in particular; a negative radius lies on the far side of the centre. It wraps a `@Sendable` function, so it is `Sendable` whatever the scalar. `Geometry.Radial(radius:)` is a circle and `Geometry.Radial(star)` is `Geometry.Star.radius(at:)`. `mixed(to:at:)` is the curve a share t of the way to another, direction by direction: r + t (r′ − r), the curve itself at 0, the other at 1, extrapolated outside them. This is how one shape morphs into another.
- `vertices(about:count:)` samples a radial curve at `count` angles 2πk / `count` about a centre, from the one on the positive x axis. `Geometry.Path(_:about:samples:joined:)` joins those samples into one closed subpath, by straight lines as `polygon(vertices:)` (`.straight`, the default) or by `loop(through:)` (`.smooth`), and is `nil` for fewer than three samples.
- A bounding box is the culling test: `boundingBox` of a polygon is tight, that of a path holds every Bézier control point and so can be larger than the curve, and `intersects(_:)` of two rectangles counts touching. A shape whose box does not intersect a rectangle has no point in it.
- `transformed(by:)` moves a point, a Bézier curve, an arc, a path segment, a subpath or a path by a `Matrix<2, 3, Scalar>` of swift-matrix, applied to (x, y, 1). `transformed(by:to:)` does the same and returns the result in another `Space`, which is how a glyph outline in font units becomes a path on a page. A bare line segment moves by `map` over its two points.
- Points, lines and Bézier control points move exactly as the matrix moves them. A path of lines and Bézier curves therefore comes back equal from the identity, and moving by `a` and then by `b` equals moving by `b.multiplied(by: a)` up to rounding. A path keeps its subpaths, their order, their closedness and each subpath's segments in order.
- An arc does not stay circular under a general affine map, so `Geometry.Arc` and `Geometry.Ellipse.Arc` both move to a `Geometry.Ellipse.Arc`, and a `.arc` segment always becomes `.ellipticalArc`, even when the map happens to keep it round. The image runs through the moved points at the same parameter: its `point(at:)` is the moved `point(at:)` of the original, up to rounding. It is stored with `semiMajor` at least `semiMinor` and neither negative, so its rotation and angles are in general not the original's, even under the identity. A map that reflects reverses the sweep. A singular map gives an arc with a zero `semiMinor`, which runs back and forth along a segment.
- `flattened(tolerance:)` returns the same subpaths, in order, with the same start points and closedness, made of lines only. Lines stay as they are. A curve becomes lines whose vertices lie on the curve, and neither the curve nor the lines stray further than the tolerance from the other. A Bézier curve is halved until every control point is within the tolerance of the chord between its ends, measured to the chord and not to the line through it. An arc is cut into equal steps whose sagitta, taken on the longer radius, is within the tolerance, and no step spans more than a quarter turn. No curve becomes more than 65536 lines; past that the tolerance is not met. A Bézier curve with a control point that is not finite becomes the line between its ends. The tolerance must be positive and finite, or the call throws `Geometry.Path.Error.tolerance`.
- `winding(around:)` is the `Winding.Number` of swift-direction: how many times the path turns around the point, counterclockwise positive. Subpaths add, and each counts as closed by a straight line back to its start whether or not it `isClosed`. Curves are not flattened for this, so the number is exact for a point off the path: a Bézier curve is halved, and an arc cut in two, only while the point is inside the box around that piece, at most 32 times, and an arc counts every turn it makes. A point on the path can fall either way. A point that is not finite has winding number zero. `contains(_:rule:)` applies a `Winding.Rule`, `nonzero` or `evenodd`, to that number. Moving path and point by the same invertible map keeps the number, and negates it when the map reflects.
- `stroked(_:tolerance:)` returns the outline of the path drawn with a `Geometry.Path.Stroke`: a width, a join and a cap. The outline is a list of closed pieces: one quadrilateral for each straight run, one piece for each join and one for each cap, subpath after subpath in order. Every piece runs counterclockwise whichever way the path runs, so filled under `nonzero` the outline is exactly the stroked region. Winding numbers in it are never negative, and they exceed one where pieces overlap, so `evenodd` does not give the region.
- Stroking flattens curves first, to the given tolerance. A vertex that flattening put inside a curve takes a round join whatever join was asked, so with round joins and caps the region is exactly the points within half the width of the flattened path, and so within the tolerance of the true stroke. Round joins and caps are arcs in the outline and are not flattened themselves.
- A `miter` join fills the corner out to the point where the two outer edges meet when that point is no further from the inner corner than `limit` times the width, which is when 1 / sin(half the angle between the two segments) is at most `limit`; otherwise it is a `bevel`, the triangle across the corner. `round` is the pie slice between the two outer edges. Where a path doubles back, `round` gives a half disc and the other two add nothing. `butt` caps add nothing, `square` caps extend each end by half the width, and `round` caps are half discs. A closed subpath is joined at its start and has no caps; an open one that returns to its start keeps its caps.
- A subpath that has segments but no length strokes to a dot: a disc for `round` caps, a square with sides along the axes for `square` caps, nothing for `butt`. A subpath with no segments strokes to nothing, and a zero width strokes every path to the empty path. A vertex that repeats the one before it, or lies at no finite distance from it, is left out before stroking. `stroked` throws `Geometry.Path.Error.width` for a negative or non-finite width, `.limit` for a miter limit below 1 or not finite, and `.tolerance` as flattening does. `Geometry.Path.Error` is `Hashable`, so it can be compared and carried inside another error that is.
- A `Geometry.Region` is a region of the plane known by its signed distance: a `ball`, a `rectangle` with rounded corners (the radius held to 0 up to half the shorter side, so `capsule(_:)` is that half), a `path` under the nonzero rule, or a `union`. `sample(at:)` gives the distance from the boundary, negative inside, and its gradient, the unit direction in which it grows fastest; the gradient is zero where there is no one such direction (a disc's centre, a point on a path). For a ball and a rectangle the distance is exact. A path counts each segment as the line between its ends, so `init(_:tolerance:)` flattens it first; its distance is to the nearest side, and costs one pass over the sides.
- `union(_:radius:)` joins its regions' boundaries with a rounded neck where they come within `radius` of each other: `Geometry.Region.Sample.joined(with:radius:)` folded over the regions in order, the smooth minimum b + (a − b)·h − r·h·(1 − h) with h = ½ + (b − a) / 2r held to 0…1, and the gradients mixed by h, which is that minimum's gradient exactly. Where two distances differ by at least the radius it is the plain minimum bit for bit, so far from a neck the union is the plain union; it is never above the plain minimum and at most a quarter of the radius below it, and it changes continuously with the regions and the radius, so two shapes that approach merge and part again smoothly. A radius that is not positive gives the plain union. `boundingBox` holds every point at no positive distance: for a union, its parts' boxes grown by a quarter of the radius; nil for a region with no points.
